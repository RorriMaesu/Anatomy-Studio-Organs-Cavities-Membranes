## Product concept and student path

Display the official title, **The Tissue Level of Organization**, with **Tissue Studio** as a secondary name. The central learning task is to identify a tissue, point to evidence, connect structure with function and recall representative locations. Avoid making the module only another numbered-label worksheet.

Keep the shared course header, chapter selector, numbered sidebar, textbook links and Learn → Explore → Practice → Review sequence. Add `/dist/chapter4/`, with durable section/activity URLs and a Chapter 4 overview in the existing library. Use a warm violet accent, restrained microscope-style focus transitions, selected-feature highlights and optional process animation. Reuse global Effects and reduced-motion settings; never animate the micrograph itself in a way that could be mistaken for biological motion.

A typical visit: choose 4.2 → learn a short classification lesson → compare the drawing and micrograph → hide overlays → identify the tissue and its visual evidence → read feedback → retry a different example. Preserve selected specimen, activity and unfinished answers on return.

## Curriculum map

### 4.1 Types of Tissues — orientation and membranes
- Four tissue families, their roles, histology and the tissue-to-organ relationship.
- Embryonic origins, the three germ layers and examples shown in the textbook; avoid oversimplified “one tissue family equals one germ layer” rules.
- Cutaneous, mucous, serous and synovial membranes; distinguish epithelial membranes from connective tissue membranes.
- Explore: a four-family tissue atlas; a germ-layer matching activity; membrane cross-sections with layer labels and body-location context.
- Practice: identify a family from structural clues, associate functions, classify membranes and explain a layer's role.
- Source figures: 4.2–4.4. Link back to Chapter 1's cavity/serous-membrane activity and Chapter 3's differentiation section.

### 4.2 Epithelial Tissue — the largest classification workshop
Use four lesson groups: (1) surfaces and junctions; (2) naming/classification; (3) function and location; (4) glands.
- Polarity, apical/basal surfaces, basal lamina and basement membrane, cellular packing, support and renewal.
- Tight, anchoring and gap junctions; distinguish their functions and illustrated examples.
- Simple versus stratified layers; squamous, cuboidal and columnar shapes; pseudostratified and transitional appearances; keratinized/nonkeratinized examples.
- Goblet cells, surface specializations, endothelium and mesothelium with carefully checked definitions.
- Endocrine/exocrine distinctions; unicellular/multicellular glands; duct and secretory-unit classification; merocrine, apocrine and holocrine secretion.
- Explore: a guided classifier that asks about layers before surface-cell shape; an epithelial sheet with clickable surfaces/junctions; a stretch-state comparison for transitional tissue; gland-route and secretion-mode animations.
- Practice: identify both the class and the evidence; compare pseudostratified versus stratified; choose function/location; label junctions; sequence secretion. Include keyboard alternatives to every sorting interaction.
- Source figures: 4.5–4.11, with 4.8 as the principal comparison reference. Figure 4.7 supports goblet-cell identification.

### 4.3 Connective Tissue Supports and Protects — cells plus matrix
Use three lesson groups: (1) cells/fibers/ground substance; (2) connective tissue proper; (3) supportive/fluid tissues.
- Connective-tissue functions and developmental introduction; mesenchyme and mucous connective tissue.
- Fibroblasts/fibrocytes, adipocytes, mesenchymal cells and the defense cells discussed in the section.
- Collagen, elastic and reticular fibers; matrix and ground substance.
- Loose: areolar, adipose and reticular. Dense: regular, irregular and elastic.
- Hyaline, elastic and fibrocartilage; bone; blood and lymph. Keep depth appropriate to Chapter 4 rather than importing entire skeletal or blood chapters.
- Explore: a clickable matrix diagram; fiber-orientation comparison with a conceptual pull-direction animation; a connective-tissue classification tree; cartilage comparison; drawing/micrograph pairs.
- Practice: classify from matrix evidence, identify cells/fibers where visible, distinguish look-alike tissues and connect structure to a plausible job and location.
- Source figures: 4.12–4.17 and Table 4.1. Clearly label any supplementary bone/lymph visual and its source rather than implying it came from a Chapter 4 figure.

### 4.4 Muscle Tissue and Motion — distinguish the three types
- Skeletal, cardiac and smooth muscle: cell shape, striations, nuclei, branching, intercalated discs, control and representative location/function.
- Excitability and contraction at the introductory level; do not expand into a detailed sarcomere physiology course.
- Explore: synchronized comparison cards and magnified structural features; a simple motion illustration for each type.
- Practice: tissue identification plus a second distinguishing clue; correct false rules such as “striated means skeletal.”
- Source figure 4.18 and Table 4.2.

### 4.5 Nervous Tissue Mediates Perception and Response — identify and trace
- Neurons and neuroglia; the glial cell types and functions introduced in the section.
- Cell body, nucleus, dendrites, axon and myelin as shown in the diagram; perception and response at Chapter 4 depth.
- Explore: labeled neuron schematic beside a micrograph; a step-through information-flow illustration; glial support cards.
- Practice: label structures, distinguish neuron/glial roles and explain a simple information-flow sequence.
- Source figures 4.19–4.20. Show only structures actually resolvable in each micrograph; do not fabricate axon/dendrite distinctions in an ambiguous image.

### 4.6 Tissue Injury and Aging — explain a process
- Inflammation and its signs; injury response, clotting and repair; regeneration versus fibrosis/scar formation.
- Necrosis versus apoptosis; effects of aging and atrophy; altered proliferation and tissue organization in cancer.
- Explore: a stepwise repair timeline with adjustable playback and captions; tissue organization comparison; a repair-outcome scenario activity.
- Practice: order events, connect signs with mechanisms, compare cell-death processes and reason about repair. Keep pathology examples educational, not diagnosis or treatment advice.
- Source figures 4.21–4.22; Figure 4.1 can support a normal/altered organization comparison.

## Figure and specimen policy

Create a figure manifest before building screens: original figure number/title, PDF page, source link, credit/license, canonical labels, image bounds, supported features and safe quiz crops. Audit all Figures 4.1–4.22 and Tables 4.1–4.2; include each as an interactive asset or linked reference with a documented role. Extract the highest useful native resolution rather than screenshotting a whole PDF page. Preserve figure-specific micrograph credits and available magnification/scale information.

Use the textbook's exact displayed labels as the answer-key authority; alternate spellings and recognized synonyms are a separate acceptance list. Do not repeat the Frons/Frontal substitution mistake. Label original diagrams at their existing callout endpoints. For micrographs, use optional, reviewed overlays on actual visible features, not dense leader lines. Never use generated imagery as a real histology specimen.

Provide drawing/micrograph switching and side-by-side comparison when space permits. On compact screens use one image with a persistent toggle, not two unreadably small panels. Fit the default figure and primary controls on desktop; deliberate zoom can pan within the image. Long terms and explanations use filters, pages or short tabs. Preserve keyboard focus across changes and provide descriptive alt text in study mode; remove answer-revealing captions and accessibility labels during assessment.

Textbook image reuse and any additional external specimens require a per-image credit/license check. Additional specimens are optional until clearly reusable examples are verified. Multiple crops of one image must not be advertised as independent unseen specimens.

## Practice, feedback and exam design

Each objective needs a short explanation, a visual/process activity where appropriate, at least two reviewed questions and a direct remediation link. Use authored questions aligned with objectives, not copied end-of-chapter questions. Proposed first-release bank: approximately 120–150 items, adjusted after the coverage audit. Suggested distribution: 4.1 15–20; 4.2 30–40; 4.3 30–40; 4.4 15; 4.5 15; 4.6 15–20. Counts are targets, not promises of completeness.

Default section practice: ten untimed mixed questions, with filters for identification, labeling, function/location, comparison and sequencing. Where useful, ask “Which tissue?” followed by “Which visible feature supports your answer?” Score these separately so guessing the name is not treated as explaining it. Mark hints/reveals assisted and preserve that flag through reload and retries. A fresh retry can earn independent credit.

Feedback: correct term → visible evidence or mechanism → why the selected distractor does not fit → exact lesson/figure link. Teach high-value contrasts explicitly: simple/pseudostratified/stratified; squamous/cuboidal; regular/irregular; hyaline/elastic/fibrocartilage; skeletal/cardiac/smooth; regeneration/fibrosis; apoptosis/necrosis. Memory cues support those distinctions and are labeled as aids, not biological rules.

Default chapter exam: 40 questions, blueprint 6/10/10/5/4/5 across sections 4.1–4.6. Include a deliberate mix of images and reasoning, randomize reproducibly, avoid duplicate targets in short exams and defer feedback until submission. If a section lacks enough verified questions, disclose reduced coverage rather than silently repeat questions. Keep one validated specimen/example outside ordinary drill where possible; if the bank is too small, call the assessment a reviewed-image exam, not unseen transfer testing.

Progress should distinguish attempted, independently correct, assisted and due-to-review questions. Show section-level evidence rather than a single unsupported “mastered” badge. Store explicit stable chapter/section/objective/specimen IDs; preserve imported/legacy records. Fold Chapter 4 into global backup, restore, reset and stale-tab protection. Reset messaging must no longer say only Chapters 1–3.
